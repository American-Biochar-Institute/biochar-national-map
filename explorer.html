The NRCS biochar rating: what the science supports

The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) gives every mapped soil a rating from 0 to 1 for how much biochar is expected to improve it. This page explains what goes into that number, and how well research backs each piece.

The short answer: the rating looks at 13 factors. Research backs the main idea behind the soil factors: biochar tends to help most in acidic, sandy, low-fertility soils; results for low-carbon and compacted soils are less consistent. But the rating is often set by a single site check. Mapped soil conditions may not reflect a field's current drainage, so on tile- or ditch-drained land the water table and ponding checks may not apply. For four factors (slope, flooding, stony ground and shrink-swell clay), no reviewed study validates the NRCS cutoffs, and the few studies that tested slope found mixed or no effects on biochar response. No reviewed study validates the exact cutoffs NRCS uses.

How the rating is worked out

NRCS built the rating for a biochar like those from corn stover or manure, with medium-sized particles, spread on the surface with little tillage. A different biochar or application method may change which factors matter.

  1. Score each soil property. Each of the seven soil properties earns from 0 to 0.15 points. A soil earns full points where it has the most room to improve, for example an acidic soil for pH.
  2. Add up the points. Seven properties at 0.15 each come to 1.05 at most. NRCS caps the total at 1.
  3. Check the site. Each of the six site checks gets a grade from 0 (fails) to 1 (no problem).
  4. Take the lower number. The rating is the lower of the points total and the worst site check. One failed check makes the rating 0, however good the soil properties are.

Drained land. Mapped soil conditions may not reflect a field's current drainage. A field with working tile, ditches or surface mounding can be much drier than the map says, so its water table and ponding checks may not apply.

What research says about each factor

Does research support the way NRCS treats each factor? Supported means studies point the same way as the rating. Depends on your field or goal means it holds for some fields or goals and not others, for example drained land. Not supported means studies point the other way. Rating rule not validated by reviewed studies means the reviewed research does not establish that the factor should lower biochar response as the NRCS rule prescribes. Related published results may exist. One answer is the same for every factor, so it is not a column: no reviewed study validates the exact cutoffs NRCS uses, such as pH 5.5 and 7.5 or slopes of 6% and 15%. Research speaks to the direction of each effect, not to where the lines sit.

Evidence: established means several research summaries agree; emerging means a few studies or summaries with mixed results; thin means little or no direct study.

What this means for you

    Take-home message

    Research supports the direction of several soil-property curves: biochar tends to do the most in acidic, sandy, low-fertility soils, while results for low-carbon and compacted soils are less consistent. The site-check half often sets the rating. Mapped soil conditions may not reflect a field's current drainage, so on drained land the water table and ponding checks may not apply, and four factors (slope, flooding, stony ground and shrink-swell clay) have no validated cutoffs as limits on what biochar does. Treat the rating as a starting point: check your drainage, use your own soil-test values, think about how you will apply biochar, and match the factors to your goal.

      Key findings

      Part 1

      How the rating is computed

      Reconstructed from the NASIS rule schematic and criteria report, then validated against published SSURGO ratings. NRCS assumes a corn-stover or manure biochar of medium particle size, surface applied with minimal tillage incorporation; a different product or placement may change which factors are relevant.

      Rating = min( water table, slope and O horizon, ponding, flooding, cobbles and stones, karst, min(1, 0.15 × [AWC + BD ratio + Ksat + LEP + MEQ + OC + pH]) )
      • Site factors are gates. The most limiting one caps the rating. The NASIS schematic also shows a subaqueous-soil gate ("SAS - Drainage Class Not Subaqueous"), which rates subaqueous soils 0; it does not occur in the Michigan data.
      • Dynamic properties are added. Each contributes up to 0.15, so the sum reaches 1.05 and is capped at 1.
      • Curves are NASIS S-curves (piecewise quadratic) between the published breakpoints, linear for slope, and class lookups for flooding, ponding and karst.
      • Classes use quarter cuts: 0 Negligible; to 0.25 Low; to 0.50 Fair; to 0.75 Good; above 0.75 Excellent.
      • Validation. With all factors on and no user data, the Institute's engine reproduces 100% of 34,821 published Michigan component ratings (largest difference 0.001). Rebuilt from SSURGO properties alone, 95.5% match exactly.
      • Mapped condition. Ratings describe soils as mapped. Mapped water table, ponding and flooding may not match a field's current drainage, and some mapped components already represent drained conditions.
      • Stored subrules. NRCS stores only the five most limiting subrules per component. Unstored dynamic values are calculated from SSURGO and calibrated so the all-on score equals the published rating.

      Part 2

      Three groups, with a working score

      The Institute's grouping of the 13 factors from the evidence in Part 3. Pick a Michigan soil component, switch factors off or enter measured values, and compare the result with the published NRCS rating. A switched-off site factor is treated as non-limiting (grade 1.0). A switched-off soil property is left out, and the properties still on share the points (0.15 × 7 / number on), so the all-on score still equals the NRCS rating and switching a property off can lower a score as well as raise it. The Institute's national map uses the same rules and the same drainage question. The adjusted score is an exploratory calculation by the Institute, not an NRCS rating.
